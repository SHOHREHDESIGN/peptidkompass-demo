An affiliate link leads to an external shop and may trigger a commission on a purchase.

Affiliate links carry an Ad label directly at the link and additionally use the sponsored nofollow attribute. The label applies even to shop links without a confirmed partnership. Partner status and a possible commission do not change how a vendor is evaluated.

Agonist

Basics

A molecule that binds a receptor and triggers its signalling chain. Opposite: antagonist.

An agonist binds to a receptor and activates the signalling chain linked to it, similar to a key unlocking a lock. The counterpart is an antagonist: it also binds the receptor but does not trigger a signalling chain, instead occupying the site and blocking an agonist from binding. Studies use both roles to probe receptor signalling pathways in a targeted way.

Aliquot

Handling

An aliquot is a small, measured portion taken from a larger laboratory solution, usually stored separately.

Rather than repeatedly thawing and refreezing a whole reconstituted solution, laboratories divide it into several aliquots. Each portion is withdrawn and used on its own while the remaining portions stay sealed. This reduces temperature cycling and contamination risk for the rest of the volume.

Amino acid

Basics

Auch: Aminosäuren

The basic building block that peptides and proteins are assembled from via peptide bonds.

An amino acid is a small molecule with an amino group and a carboxyl group. When several link together via peptide bonds, a chain forms that becomes a peptide or a protein depending on its length. Twenty different amino acids occur naturally in the construction of peptides and proteins, and their order in the chain is called the sequence.

Analogue

Basics

Auch: Analoga

A synthetically modified variant of an endogenous peptide with a slightly altered structure.

An analogue is a lab-made variant of a naturally occurring peptide in which individual amino acids have been swapped, added or removed. Researchers use such structural changes to study properties like stability or receptor binding behaviour in a targeted way. One example is semaglutide, an analogue of the endogenous hormone GLP-1.

Antimicrobial peptides

Compound classes

Auch: AMPs, Wirtsabwehrpeptide

Antimicrobial peptides are part of innate immune defence, produced in skin and mucous membranes.

Antimicrobial peptides, also called AMPs or host defence peptides, are part of innate immune defence. They are produced by skin and mucosal cells as well as immune cells and insert into the membranes of bacteria, fungi or viruses. The best-known human group is the cathelicidins, whose only active human form is LL-37. Beyond this direct membrane mechanism, AMPs are also studied as immune system signalling molecules.

Bacteriostatic Water

Handling

Auch: BAC-Wasser

Bacteriostatic water is sterile water with a preservative, usually benzyl alcohol, for solutions used more than once.

The added preservative, typically 0.9 per cent benzyl alcohol, inhibits bacterial growth in an opened solution. This lets a vial be used across several withdrawals in the lab instead of being discarded after a single use. Where no preservative is tolerated, sterile water without additives is used instead.

Bioregulators

Compound classes

Auch: Peptid-Bioregulatoren, Khavinson-Peptide

Bioregulators are very short synthetic peptides of two to four amino acids studied at organ systems.

Bioregulators are a class of very short peptides, usually two to four amino acids long, developed at a Soviet institute for gerontology in the 1970s. The underlying hypothesis is that such short sequences interact with gene regulation in an organ-specific way. A well-known example is the tetrapeptide epitalon, studied in ageing research. The evidence base comes largely from Russian-language publications.

Bioavailability

Basics

The proportion of a substance that reaches the bloodstream unchanged and can be measured there.

Bioavailability indicates what proportion of an administered substance ultimately reaches systemic circulation unchanged, measured as a percentage. It depends partly on how much the substance is broken down by enzymes, such as through proteolysis, on its way through the body. Pharmacokinetic studies determine bioavailability separately for different routes of administration.

Blind test

Laboratory

In a blind test, a sample is sent to a lab with no indication of the manufacturer or shop, so the result stays unbiased.

Because the lab does not know the origin or sender, a possible bias in the result can be ruled out. A blind test checks the actual content of one specific sample, not the origin or authenticity of the batch itself.

Auch: Batch, Los

A batch is a single production run with its own batch number, the one a CoA actually refers to.

Chromatogram

Laboratory

A chromatogram is the graphical output of an HPLC run, where each peak represents one component of the sample.

The area under the main peak gives the stated purity value, while smaller peaks represent impurities or degradation products. Without an additional identity check, the chromatogram alone does not show which molecule the main peak actually is.

Documentation score

Comparison

The documentation score shows how many verified vendor claims are actually documented.

The score combines five weighted criteria: lab 25 percent, legal framework 25 percent, shipping 15 percent, range 15 percent and price 20 percent. Missing criteria are not counted as zero points, they are omitted and the remaining weights are redistributed. It is not a quality seal or a legal assessment.

Double-blind trial

Studies

Auch: Doppelverblindung, doppelblind

A study design where neither participants nor staff know who gets the active substance or placebo. Reduces unconscious bias.

In a double-blind trial, the assignment to the treatment or control group is hidden from both participants and the study staff treating them. This prevents expectations on either side from influencing the result or its assessment. Together with randomisation and placebo control, double blinding counts as a strong quality marker.

DSIP

Peptide

Auch: Delta Sleep Inducing Peptide, Delta Sleep Peptide

One of the longest-studied neuropeptides, originally isolated as a sleep-inducing messenger from the brain of sleeping rabbits, with an inconsistent body of research to this day.

DSIP (delta sleep-inducing peptide) is a nonapeptide first isolated in 1977 by Schoenenberger and Monnier from the venous brain blood of sleeping rabbits and named for its ability to trigger delta-wave EEG activity in that animal model. Over the following decades it was studied in numerous human trials on sleep, the stress axis, and hormone regulation. The originally described sleep-promoting effect has not been reliably reproduced in independent, more rigorous studies.

Fragment

Basics

A short section of a larger endogenous peptide or protein, synthetically reproduced.

A fragment covers only a section of the original amino acid chain of a peptide or protein, not the full sequence. Such partial sequences are synthesised to study which part of a chain is responsible for particular properties, such as receptor binding. One known example is thymosin alpha-1, a fragment of a larger precursor protein.

Laboratory

A counter ion is a peptide's salt partner, usually trifluoroacetate (TFA) or acetate, left over from manufacturing.

Peptides are rarely supplied as the pure substance alone, but usually as a salt form with a bound counter ion. This share counts toward the total weight of the powder, which lowers the calculated net peptide content. TFA and acetate salts are the most common forms.

H

Half-life

Basics

The time it takes for a substance's concentration in the body to drop by half.

Half-life describes how long it takes for a peptide's blood concentration to fall to half its starting value. It is measured in pharmacokinetic studies and varies considerably between individual peptides, partly depending on how quickly endogenous enzymes break down the chain through proteolysis. A shorter half-life means faster breakdown compared with a longer one.

In vitro

Studies

Auch: in vitro

A test outside a living organism, usually on cell cultures. It says nothing about an effect in humans.

In vitro studies examine isolated cells or molecules in a controlled setting such as a petri dish. They can show early biochemical effects, but the cell context does not replace a whole organism. This kind of data counts as lab research only and does not translate to humans.

In vivo

Studies

Auch: in vivo

A test inside a living organism, usually an animal model. Closer to reality than a cell culture, but still no proof for humans.

In vivo studies observe a whole organism with metabolism, immune system and organs, usually an animal model. This gives more realistic clues than a cell culture. Because metabolism and physiology differ, animal results do not transfer directly to humans.

L

Storage

Handling

Storage describes how a peptide is kept before use, usually cool, dry and dark depending on its state.

Lyophilised powder stays considerably more stable under cool, dark and dry storage than a reconstituted solution does. Manufacturers state specific temperature ranges on the label or datasheet, and storage follows those figures. Heat, moisture and light are the three factors that change a sample's condition over time the most.

Light Protection

Handling

Light protection covers measures that shield a sample from light exposure, such as amber vials or storage in the dark.

Some peptides are sensitive to UV and visible light, which can encourage breakdown of the molecular structure over time. Amber glass vials, light-blocking outer packaging or storage in a closed drawer are common light-protection measures in the lab. Combined with cool storage, these give lyophilised material its longest stability.

Lyophilisate

Handling

Lyophilisate is the freeze-dried powder form of a peptide, the most stable storage form before reconstitution.

Lyophilisate is produced when a dissolved peptide sample is frozen and then dried under vacuum in the laboratory, so the water passes directly from a solid to a gas. The result is a powder or a loose cake inside the vial. In this form a peptide stays stable for much longer than in solution and is only reconstituted shortly before use.

Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis pools the data of several already published studies on the same topic and evaluates them together using statistical methods. Compound classes

Auch: Mitochondrien-Peptide

Mitochondrial peptides target cellular energy production or originate from mitochondrial DNA.

Mitochondrial peptides are not a single substance class in the classical sense. The term groups together peptides that either bind at the inner mitochondrial membrane or are encoded by mitochondrial rather than nuclear DNA. The second group includes a short peptide derived from the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene, studied in metabolic research. Another example binds cardiolipin in the inner membrane and is used in research on cellular ageing and energy metabolism.

A mitochondrially encoded peptide studied as an exercise mimetic in metabolic research.

MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded directly in mitochondrial DNA rather than in the cell nucleus. Neuropeptide

Basics

A peptide that acts as a signalling molecule between cells in the nervous system.

Neuropeptides are peptides released by nerve cells that act as signalling molecules within the nervous system. They differ from classic neurotransmitters in their larger molecular structure and also bind to specific receptors on target cells. In research, neuropeptides are used among other things to map signalling pathways in the central nervous system.

Nootropics

Compound classes

Auch: Smart Drugs

Nootropics are a broad substance class studied in relation to memory, learning or stress processing.

Nootropics is an umbrella term for a very heterogeneous substance class ranging from vitamins to plant extracts to synthetic peptides, studied in relation to memory, learning ability, attention or stress processing. Within peptide research, this includes ACTH-derived fragments and tuftsin analogues, among others. The term was coined in 1972 by Romanian chemist Corneliu Giurgea. Most peptide nootropics are supported mainly by preclinical and Russian-language data so far.

Basics

Di-, tri- and oligopeptide

Auch: Dipeptid, Tripeptid

A short amino acid chain, classified by length as di-, tri- or oligopeptide.

Chain length sets the name at the shorter end of the scale. Two amino acids form a dipeptide, three a tripeptide. Chains of up to roughly ten amino acids are generally called oligopeptides. From around ten up to roughly 50 amino acids, the term is polypeptide, and beyond that, once the chain has a fixed three-dimensional shape, it becomes a protein.

Peptide

Basics

Auch: Peptide

A short chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.

A peptide is made up of several amino acids strung together by peptide bonds. Two amino acids form a dipeptide, three a tripeptide, and up to roughly 50 is called a polypeptide. Above 50 amino acids, once the chain settles into a fixed three-dimensional shape, a peptide becomes a protein. Peptides sit on the same scale as proteins, just smaller and often still unfolded.

Peptide bond

Basics

The chemical link that joins two amino acids into a chain.

A peptide bond forms when the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of the next, releasing a water molecule in the process. This link repeats along the entire chain and forms the backbone of peptides and proteins. The order of the amino acids joined this way sets the sequence.

Peptide hormone

Basics

A hormone the body produces that is chemically an amino acid chain.

A peptide hormone is a signalling molecule built from a chain of amino acids, unlike steroid hormones, which are based on cholesterol. Peptide hormones are produced in glands or specialised cells and bind to receptors on target cells. Insulin is a well-known example of an endogenous peptide hormone.

Protein

Basics

Auch: Eiweiß

A long amino acid chain with a fixed three-dimensional shape, larger than a peptide.

Like a peptide, a protein is made of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, but it is substantially longer, usually above 50 amino acids, and folds into a fixed three-dimensional shape. That folding is what sets a protein apart from a peptide, which is often still unfolded. Both terms describe points on the same size scale rather than a fundamentally different molecule.

Proteolysis

Basics

The enzymatic breakdown of an amino acid chain into smaller fragments.

Proteolysis is the process by which enzymes called proteases cleave the peptide bonds in an amino acid chain, breaking it into smaller fragments. This breakdown happens continuously in the body and affects how long a peptide stays intact, in other words its half-life. Studies often report proteolytic stability as a measure of a peptide's durability.
