Agonist

Basics

A molecule that binds a receptor and triggers its signalling chain. Opposite: antagonist.

An agonist binds to a receptor and activates the signalling chain linked to it, similar to a key unlocking a lock. The counterpart is an antagonist: it also binds the receptor but does not trigger a signalling chain, instead occupying the site and blocking an agonist from binding. Studies use both roles to probe receptor signalling pathways in a targeted way.

An aliquot is a separated sample portion. Separate portions can avoid repeated temperature cycling of the whole stock.

Amino acid

Basics

Also: Amino acids

The basic building block that peptides and proteins are assembled from via peptide bonds.

Amino acids contain an amino group and a carboxyl group. Proteins are commonly described using 20 standard amino acids. Peptide bonds join them into chains; their order is called the sequence.

Analogue

Basics

Also: Analogues

A synthetically modified variant of an endogenous peptide with a slightly altered structure.

An analogue is a lab-made variant of a naturally occurring peptide in which individual amino acids have been swapped, added or removed. Researchers use such structural changes to study properties like stability or receptor binding behaviour in a targeted way. One example is semaglutide, an analogue of the endogenous hormone GLP-1.

Animal model

Studies

A laboratory animal, such as a mouse or rat, used to study a substance in vivo. Not direct proof for humans.

An animal model is a laboratory animal such as a mouse, rat or zebrafish in which metabolism, behaviour or tissue is studied under controlled conditions. It serves preclinical research before a possible clinical trial. Because genetics and physiology differ, an effect in an animal model is not proof of an effect in humans.

Antimicrobial peptides

Compound classes

Also: AMPs, Host defence peptides

Antimicrobial peptides are part of innate immune defence, produced in skin and mucous membranes.

Antimicrobial peptides, also called AMPs or host defence peptides, are part of innate immune defence. They are produced by skin and mucosal cells as well as immune cells and insert into the membranes of bacteria, fungi or viruses. The best-known human group is the cathelicidins, whose only active human form is LL-37. Beyond this direct membrane mechanism, AMPs are also studied as immune system signalling molecules.

Laboratory

Also: Batch, Lot

A batch is a single production run with its own batch number, the one a CoA actually refers to.

The batch number links one particular vial to the matching report, which is what makes a result traceable. A CoA with no clear batch reference cannot be tied to an actual product and says correspondingly little.

Bioavailability

Basics

The proportion of a substance that reaches the bloodstream unchanged and can be measured there.

Bioavailability indicates what proportion of an administered substance ultimately reaches systemic circulation unchanged, measured as a percentage. It depends partly on how much the substance is broken down by enzymes, such as through proteolysis, on its way through the body. Pharmacokinetic studies determine bioavailability separately for different routes of administration.

In parts of peptide research, this term describes short sequences investigated for involvement in gene regulation. It is not a regulatory category establishing efficacy. Individual cellular findings establish neither organ-specific benefits nor extension of human lifespan.

Laboratory

Copper peptides

Compound classes

Also: GHK-Cu complexes

Copper peptides are complexes of a small peptide and a copper ion, the best known being GHK-Cu.

Copper peptides are compounds in which a small peptide coordinates a copper ion. The best-known example is GHK, a three-amino-acid peptide found in human blood plasma, saliva and urine, where it naturally occurs bound to copper. In its copper-complex form, GHK-Cu is studied mainly in skin research, with additional preclinical data on connective tissue and wound healing.

GHRH

Compound classes

Growth hormone-releasing hormone

Also: Somatoliberin

GHRH is a hypothalamic hormone that triggers the pituitary gland to release growth hormone.

Growth hormone-releasing hormone is a hypothalamic peptide that travels via the pituitary portal system to the pituitary gland, where it triggers growth hormone release from somatotroph cells. GHRP

Compound classes

Growth hormone-releasing peptide

Also: GH secretagogues, Growth hormone secretagogues

GHRPs are synthetic peptides that trigger growth hormone release through the ghrelin receptor.

Growth hormone-releasing peptides are a class of synthetic peptides that bind the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R) to trigger growth hormone release from the pituitary gland. Unlike GHRH analogues, they use a separate signalling pathway and are often combined with GHRH analogues in research. The ghrelin receptor is also involved in regulating hunger and appetite.

GIP

Compound classes

Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide

GIP is a gut hormone that regulates insulin release after carbohydrate-rich meals.

Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide is the second major incretin hormone alongside GLP-1. K cells in the upper small intestine release it after eating, and it binds the GIP receptor to regulate insulin release. It is also involved in fat metabolism. Several newer diabetes and obesity compounds combine GIP with GLP-1 receptor agonism.

GLP-1 is a gut hormone released after eating that regulates insulin release.

Growth hormone

Compound classes

Somatotropin

Also: GH, HGH, Somatotropin

Growth hormone is a pituitary hormone that regulates growth, metabolism and body composition.

Growth hormone, also called somatotropin or GH, is produced by somatotroph cells in the pituitary gland and released into the blood in pulses, controlled by GHRH, somatostatin and ghrelin. H

Half-life

Basics

The time it takes for a substance's concentration in the body to drop by half.

Half-life describes how long it takes for a peptide's blood concentration to fall to half its starting value. It is measured in pharmacokinetic studies and varies considerably between individual peptides, partly depending on how quickly endogenous enzymes break down the chain through proteolysis. A shorter half-life means faster breakdown compared with a longer one.

Incretins are gut hormones released after eating that are involved in regulating insulin release.

Incretins are a hormone class from the gut that includes GLP-1 and GIP. They are released from specialised gut cells after eating and bind their own receptors on pancreatic beta cells. The International Unit measures a substance's biological potency against a reference standard, not its weight.

Meta-analysis

Studies

Peptide bond

Basics

The chemical link that joins two amino acids into a chain.

A peptide bond forms when the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of the next, releasing a water molecule in the process. This link repeats along the entire chain and forms the backbone of peptides and proteins. The order of the amino acids joined this way sets the sequence.
