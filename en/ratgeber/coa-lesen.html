How to read a peptide COA: purity, amount and identity

“99% pure” sounds definitive. Without the method, batch attribution and an amount measurement, much remains unclear. A certificate of analysis, or COA, becomes useful when you read its tests separately.

Three questions, three different checks

Identity: what was detected?

Identity tests compare characteristic sample properties with a reference. A report may use spectra or retention times, for example. One method may not distinguish every similar substance.

Purity: what does the measurement profile show?

Chromatographic purity describes the relationship between evaluated signals under the stated measurement conditions. It is not a blanket assurance that every possible impurity has been excluded.

Amount: how much was measured?

An amount determination or assay may report a value in mg. Total powder weight, compound amount and purity percentage are not the same. Water, salts and additives can contribute to total weight.

How to check a report

  1. Match the sample

    Compare product name, batch, sample identifier and date. A report for a different batch is not evidence for the listing you are assessing.

  2. Open the original

    Open the complete document and a laboratory verification page where available. A cropped image may omit methods, limitations or other findings. A logo alone does not establish authenticity.

  3. Read the method and unit

    Check whether a value is expressed as a percentage, mg or another unit. Match it to the actual test. “Pass” is meaningful only in relation to the test target and limit.

  4. Identify the gaps

    When a result is absent, record it as “not established”. Missing information proves neither a pass nor a failure.

What even a detailed COA cannot establish

A report initially concerns the tested sample. It does not automatically establish who collected it, whether it represented the whole batch or how each later vial was stored. These are questions of sampling, traceability and supply chain.

Purity testing, microbial counts and endotoxin testing are also different investigations. One result cannot stand in for a test that was not performed. A COA is not a clinical safety assessment or evidence of medical efficacy.

Common questions about peptide COAs

Does 99% purity mean 9.9 mg in a 10 mg pack?

You cannot make that calculation without additional information. A chromatographic percentage is not automatically the mass fraction of the entire powder. Establishing the amount requires an appropriate quantitative measurement.

Does HPLC automatically establish identity?

No. The procedure, references and interpretation matter. Retention time, spectra and additional methods provide different information. Read which identity test the report actually lists.

Is a COA link enough to establish a good supplier?

It is one piece of evidence. Traceable batch attribution, verifiable company information, clear pricing and shipping terms also matter. A partnership with our portal replaces none of these checks.
